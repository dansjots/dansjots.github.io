Remarks

Experimented with setting up an always-running yggmail server that I connect to via Tailscale.

Had to figure out that you need to explicitly map the tcp ports on your Tailnet via tailscale serve, since the mail server listens on two ports (1025 and 1134 for SMTP and IMAP by default, respectively)

tailscale serve --bg --tcp 1143 1143
tailscale serve --bg --tcp 1025 1025
yggmail -peer=<address to your choice of yggmail peer>

I use DeltaChat as the client on my devices, which is configured as follows:

E-Mail Address: <your yggmail pubic key>@yggmail
Existing Password: ********

Inbox:
IMAP Login Name: <leave to default>
IMAP Server: <name of your server on the tailnet>
IMAP Port: 1143
IMAP Security: Off

Outbox:
SMTP Login Name: <leave to default>
SMTP Password: <leave to default>
SMTP Server: <name of your server on the tailnet>
SMTP Port: 1025
SMTP Security: Off
Certificate Checks: <leave to default>

Software instructions tends be rather feast-or-famine:

  • Case 1: you’re a “technical user”, in which case you’re expected to already possess all prerequisite mental models of the referenced shorthands mentioned in subsequent instructions (or to slog through endless indirections of also jargon-laden sources all expecting the same) with no attempts made at explaining the context or rationale.
  • Case 2: you’re “non-technical”, in which case you’re treated as total invalids with no ability to comprehend anything more complex than a faucet, and everything is designed to insulate you from any glimpse of plumbing whatsoever, all possible escape hatch sealed off with babyproof foam lest you cut yourself on sharp edges or get confused and lose patience.

This dichotomy results in the neglect of a middleground demographic of non-technical but intelligent power users. They may lack prior domain-specific knowledge, but are fully capable of understanding the concepts when instructed patiently.

Such demographic are willing to go through some degree of complexity, if only you be upfront and brief them on the kinds of rough edges they may expect to encounter and most importantly the context behind why it is that way.

In my opinion, instead of “keep polishing until it’s end-user ready” and pretending that rough edges don’t exist at all as long as you stay on the golden path, that effort is better-spent directed towards trying bring them onto the same page as yourself.

The key is to give them a sense of agency, such that even when hiccups occur they at least feel being in the driver’s seat.

Saw a discussion today mentioning Einstein’s proof of the Pythagorean theorem via subdividing areas.

In the process of working it out on paper in my own preferred notation, I realized that the proof could in fact be streamlined such that it could be included alongside the initial introduction of the theorem without adding much cognitive overhead.

This is actually the very first time that I’ve looked at any proofs of the theorem at all, so I was certain that something so simple must’ve already been conceived in the past, and sure enough it’s right among the very foremost proofs frequently included in most materials. However, for whatever reason, all of them uses an archaic form of notation that is very hard to parse mentally and, in my opinion, obfuscates the simplicity of the proof by adding unnecessary cognitive overhead.

So that’s the motivation for writing it up in my preferred notation, mainly as a record for myself to refer back to in the future.
